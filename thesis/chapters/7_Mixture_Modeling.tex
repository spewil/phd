\section{Mixtures of Gaussians}

Gaussian mixture models (GMMs) are used to model distributions with multimodality, where the generating process underlying the data is composed of multiple modes of activity arranged across the dimensions of sampled space. In our task, we peer through a relatively brief window in time to view a process of activation along the spatial dimensions of our recorded EMG signal. As subjects learn, we hypothesize that they will identify modes of motor activity, so-called mixture policies. Subjects attempt to discover, refine, and reliably recall these policies in order to collect greater amounts of reward. This framing of our task implies that overall motor variability not only decreases across learning, as we have shown, but also separates into individual modes which comprise a subjects' policies.

Gaussian mixture models are probability distributions of the form
%
\begin{align}
  {\displaystyle p({ {\theta }} | {x} )=\sum _{i=1}^{K}\phi _{i}{\mathcal {N}}({ {\mu _{i},\Sigma _{i}}})}
\end{align}
%
where ${x}$ is the data over which the model is conditioned, ${\theta}$ is the set of parameters of the model, and $K$ is the chosen number of mixture modes or components; $\phi_i$, ${\mu_i}$, and ${\Sigma_i}$ are the weight, mean, and covariance of mixture mode $i$.

% PDF when transforming gaussian rv to log-gaussian rv https://stats.stackexchange.com/questions/214997/multivariate-log-normal-probabiltiy-density-function-pdf
% PDF when exponentiating gaussian to get lognormal? gauhttps://stats.stackexchange.com/questions/89970/exponential-of-a-standard-normal-random-variable

Here we use Gaussian mixtures not to find a model which generalizes well to new data as one would seek in, for example, a classification problem. Instead, we use mixture models as a tool to capture statistics of our datasets across time. For this purpose, we choose fit hyperparameters based on our prior knowledge and hold these constant for direct comparisons across subjects and conditions.

To fit our Gaussian mixture models, we use the variational Bayesian method in order to influence the fits through the choice of prior distributions. We constrain the models to 12 mixture modes, which reflects our intuition for the solution to the task: 12 unique modal solutions, one for each target. For a prior on the component weights, we chose, after experimentation, a Dirichlet process prior with a low weight concentration parameter. These parameters encourage, but do not force, mixture weights which are sparse, biasing the model towards fewer, more weighted components. In practice we find that, despite this encouragement, component weights are seldom set near zero when fitting. This reflects the dimensionality of the problem (64 dimensions of EMG), where activity in the relatively high dimensional space is sparse leading to more modes remaining active when fitting. All data is log-transformed before fitting to take advantage of the log-normality of the EMG data. To ``pull back'' the fit to the original lognormal EMG space we can exponentially transform the mean and covariance of each GMM mode using
%
\begin{align}
  \mu_i^l &= e^{{\mu}_i + \frac{1}{2}\Sigma_{ii}}\\
  \Sigma_{ij}^l &= \left(e^{\mu_i + \mu_j + \frac{1}{2}\left(\Sigma_{ii} + \Sigma_{jj}\right)}\right)\left(e^{\Sigma_{ij}} - 1\right).
  % cov[i,j] = (np.exp(normal_mean[i] + normal_mean[j] + 0.5*(normal_covariance[i,i] + normal_covariance[j,j]))*(np.exp(normal_covariance[i,j]) - 1))
\end{align}
%
where $\mu_i^l$ and $\Sigma_{ij}^l$ are the lognormal moments while the other parameters are as defined in the GMM model. Note that when we sample from our fitted models, we sample from distributions defined in log-transformed space and exponentiate these samples to pull back to the lognormal space.

In \Cref{fig:gmm_vs_pca}, we compare PCA to our mixture model fits for a representative subject's first group of target task trials. We compare the reconstructed covariance of the principal subspace, the covariance of each mixture model component, and the sample covariance of the data. In the PCA column (right), we visualize the top three principal components as rank-1 covariances, noting the singular value magnitude of each. PCA, by design, extracts ``broader'', smoother components in an attempt to reconstruct the broad space of activity, reflected in the ``offset''-like character of the top component, with many active channels. Subsequent PCA components hone in on groups of active channels to reflect mode-like activations. In the bottom row we confirm that PCA reconstructs the empirical covariance well with few components, as seen earlier in the subspace confinement comparisons. Gaussian mixtures naturally find more specific aspects of the dataset, as reflected in the covariances of the three highest weighted mixture components shown in left column. Mixture models provide a finer grained structure of subjects' movement ``policies'', yielding statistics of the EMG distributions that we can compare across subjects and across time.

For a quantitative measure of goodness of fit for our models, we compare the log-likelihoods of mixture models fit with 12 components with a single Gaussian as a means of assessing any failures of mixture models to converge to a solution. In \Cref{fig:model_likelihood}, we plot the log-likelihood of a Gaussian fit against the log-likelihood of a mixture model fit with 12 components and find that the mixture model results in a higher likelihood for all subjects across all blocks of trials across time. On one hand, it is unsurprising that adding parameters to the model increases its likelihood. On the other hand, models failing to properly fit the data would likely fail this confirmatory step, a result that this analysis rejects. In \Cref{fig:likelihood_over_components}, we assess the number of mixture components and their likelihoods by fitting an increasing number of mixture components to a representative subject's target task data. We find that as the number of components increases, the likelihood increases monotonically. We find diminishing returns to the model likelihood as the number of components increases beyond 12 components. This behavior is common across subjects.

\section{Mixture Entropy \& Total Variance}

We can use fitted mixture model distributions to estimate the entropy of subjects' data across learning. Entropy is a measure of uncertainty and information content. There is no closed-form expression for the entropy $h(X)$ of a Gaussian mixture random variable, but a lower bound $h_l(X)$ on the entropy\cite{gershmanNonparametricVariationalInference,huberEntropyApproximationGaussian2008} can be derived using Jensen's inequality:
%
% \begin{align}
%   h(\mathcal{G}) &\geq h_l(\mathcal{G}) \\ 
%   h(\mathcal{G}) &= h\left[\sum_{i}^{k}{w_i\mathcal{N}(\mu_i,\Sigma_i)}\right]\\
%   h_l(\mathcal{G}) &= \sum_i^k{w_i h(\mathcal{N}(\mu_i,\Sigma_i))} \\
%   &= \sum_i^k{w_i \left[\frac{1}{2}\log{(|\Sigma_i|)} + \frac{d}{2}\left(1 + \log(2\pi e)\right)\right]}.
% \end{align}
% 
\begin{align}
  H(q) &= - \int_\theta{q(\theta)\log{q(\theta)}d\theta} \\
       &= - \int_\theta{q(\theta)\log{\left\lbrack{\sum_j^N{w_j\,\mathcal{N}\left(\theta\,|\,\mu_i,\Sigma_i\right)}}\right\rbrack}d\theta} \\
       &\geq -\sum_{i}^{N}w_i\log\left\lbrack\sum_{j}^{M}{w_j\int_{\theta}\mathcal{N}\left(\theta\,|\,\mu_i,\Sigma_i\right)\mathcal{N}\left(\theta\,|\,\mu_j,\Sigma_j\right)d\theta}\right\rbrack \\ 
       &= -\sum_{i}^{N}w_i\log\left\lbrack\sum_{j}^{N}{w_j\,\mathcal{N}\left(\mu_i\,|\,\mu_j,\Sigma_i+\Sigma_j\right)}\right\rbrack.
\end{align}
%
In the last step, we simplify the convolution of two Gaussians using the fact that the result is also Gaussian. The higher the entropy, the less specific the activations of subjects will be. At the extreme, minimizing entropy of the mixture takes the form of delta functions in EMG space for non-overlapping, perfectly precise movements. One can imagine an ideal subject choosing exactly correct EMG activations every trial, conditioned on the target. That is, the lower bound provides us with a best-case uncertainty based on a subject's statistics. This allows us to compare across trials and subjects that subject's performance in terms of their uncertainty. Along with the entropy, the total variance, defined as the weighted sum of the covariance trace or the sum of the covariance eigenvalues, is a measure of uncertainty. This sum will reflect spatial dimensions of high variance, providing a measure of total spread across the mixture model:
%
\begin{align}
  % e(\mathcal{G}) &=  \sum_i^k{w_i \log{(|\Sigma_i|^{1/d})}} \\
  v(\mathcal{G}) &=  \sum_i^k{w_i \mathrm{tr}(\Sigma_i)/d}
\end{align}\label{eq:mixture_metrics}.
%

In \Cref{fig:gmm_entropies}, we find that the calibration dataset has the highest entropy on average over subjects, while movement and the last block of target task trials are the lowest. This agrees with our intuition of these tasks: the calibration tasks is designed to reward search and exploration, while the natural movement task is composed of well-honed actions. The target task shows a reduction in entropy from the first block to the last block. In \Cref{fig:gmm_variances}, we find a similar trend with greater difference between the first trial block and last trial blocks. We also find a difference between the natural movement model variance and the later trial blocks. This implies that on average over subjects, the later trials are even less variable than the movement trials. Task reward reinforces decreased variability across all spatial dimensions of the task compared to an ``open-loop'' task without reinforcement, as in the movement block.

Comparing entropy and variance to reward, we hypothesize that greater entropy in the calibration task will be predictive of higher reward, as the ability of a subject to ``cover'' more of the space, in effect placing probability mass across dimensions of EMG activity, is predictive of an ability to ``cover'' EMG activities which are task-relevant and rewarding. We expect higher performing subjects to have lower entropy as they move towards the ideal mixture of delta functions solution. In terms of variance, we expect to recover our earlier findings from PCA, where higher performers tend to show lower overall spatial variance in their target task trials. We expect little predictive power from the movement dataset, while higher variance in the calibration dataset may imply greater ability to reach across spatial dimensions of EMG, in accordance with the argument for entropy.

In \Cref{fig:gmm_entropy_vs_reward}, we find a weak correlation between entropy and reward for the calibration as well as the first block of target task trials. We interpret this result as relating subjects' ability to decorrelate their activity in the calibration task, to maximally explore in first block of the target task to find rewarding EMG activations. In \Cref{fig:gmm_variance_vs_reward}, however, we find no correlation between total variance and reward. This implies that higher performing subjects are no more variable, in terms of the total variance of their mixture model components, than lower performers, while the utility of the variability of higher performers is indicative of higher rewarding movements. That is, variability is not enough-- variance along a variety of task dimensions is predictive of reward. This is akin to idea of ``maximum entropy'' policies in reinforcement learning, or entropy regularization in general machine learning, where it is advantageous to maximize entropy in order to maintain exploration while balancing this maximization with taking rewarding actions.

Early in learning, higher entropy mixtures are indicative of greater mean reward as these are rewarding searches in EMG space as in the calibration case. Meanwhile, lower variability implies greater reward as subjects are who less generally variable are more likely to reach rewarding activations as opposed to noise. In this way, entropy and total variance build a complementary picture of subjects which are able to make meaningful but exploratory actions, balancing exploration and exploitation, will be more performant. 

In \Cref{fig:gmm_entropy_diffs} and \Cref{fig:gmm_variance_diffs}, we visualize the mean of the differences in entropy and total variance between blocks of trials shown in prior figures. This works as a coarse mean over the derivative; negative values indicate reduction over time, while the magnitude indicates the rate of reduction. We find that higher performers tend to decrease total variance over learning, confirming prior results, while also reducing entropy over time. These results are in accordance with our working theory that performance in the task results in an overall spatial reduction in variability and thus uncertainty in movements. This applies, on average, across spatial dimensions of EMG. Subjects identify solutions to their task, and work to reliably repeat those solutions. The action distribution conditioned on a particular target narrows across learning, both in task space and within the ambient EMG space.

\section{Mixture Subspace Confinement}

In \Cref{chap:data_manifold}, we visualized the variance captured by the two-dimensional principal subspace of each task and trial block which we termed the ``subspace confinement'' to reflect the measurement of complexity of the dataset. This complexity was based on the degree to which a subject is successful in finding a plane of maximum variance in the data. For comparison, we compute the same metric here, taking a weighted mean over the mixture components. In the context of the entropy and total variance metrics used in this chapter, subspace confinement provides an intermediate value related to the average magnitude of the top two eigenvalues of the mixture component covariances. 

We are now separating the data into Gaussian components, rather than analyzing the data as a single Gaussian. We hypothesize that these modes, as they provide greater expressiveness to the model, to be less subspace confined on average than a single Gaussian. This is due to each fitted mode having its own variance in orthogonal directions as opposed a single basis fit to the entire dataset. This provides a clearer picture of the spatial variability within each mode. As noted in \Cref{chap:data_manifold}, we find that each mode's principal manifold displays greater sphericity compared to the overall dataset, where a few dimensions of variance tend to dominate.

% Connect back to the toy mode \Cref{fig:toy_model} and the concept of ``effective rank''. That this is a proxy for the rank of the components which make up the mixture of modes in the dataset. PCA can't unmix these modes, so we use GMMs to try to unmix.

These intuitions are confirmed in \Cref{fig:gmm_rank},where we find that subspace confinement, or effective rank, within modes is lower compared to a single Gaussian model. The calibration dataset is significantly higher in average confinement across subjects than all other tasks. This agrees with the design of the calibration task, where subjects attempt to maximally decorrelate their EMG activity, and the mixture model captures those low-rank (less spherical) modes. In the target task, subjects' modes become on average less subspace constrained across learning. We interpret this as a progression of subjects' activity modes ``settling'' into solutions, and thus the structure of that variability becoming more spherical around those solutions. As we will explore in the next chapter, this implies that while total variance (as shown in \Cref{fig:gmm_variances}) decreases over learning, the structure of this variability broadens spatially, as opposed to becoming more focused on particular spatial dimensions of variance. Across learning, the variance of each mode evolves from higher magnitudes across a few spatial dimensions to more concentrated and shared more equally across dimensions.

In \Cref{fig:rank_vs_reward}, we plot subspace confinement of each model for each subject against subjects' mean reward. We hypothesized that subjects with lower confinement would earn increased reward, as those subjects are less constrained in their task of target discovery. This is supported by the negative correlation between the mean effective rank of each target task model and reward. The degree to which subjects' solutions are less subspace constrained is predictive of higher performance. We find no strong effect in the movement or calibration datasets, reflecting the fact that the spatial variance in these tasks is decoupled from the target task reward as designed.

\section{Optimal Transport of Mixtures over Learning}

Using our mixture model parameters, we can ask what changes in the space of these parameters across learning, and are these changes common across subjects? We hypothesize that subjects on average would become more self-similar over learning in terms of their mixture component means and covariances. We also hypothesize that higher-performing subjects would show greater similarity over learning, as lower performing subjects would maintain a degree of variability in their movements as they continued their search in solution space.

The simplest method of computing differences across mixtures is directly in terms of means and covariances. For each pair of mixture models compared, say $i$ and $j$, the closest pairs of component means in EMG space from within each mixture are found by comparing all means between the two mixtures and finding $\min_{ij}||\mu^0_i - \mu^1_j||^2$. By pairing components across the two mixtures, we can compute the Frobenius norm of the difference between those pairs of covariances and take the mean over component pairs to find a mean distance. The Frobenius norm of the difference between two square matrices $A$ and $B$ of dimension $n$ is
%
\begin{align}
  ||A-B||_F = \left(\sum_{i,j}^{n}{||a_{ij} - b_{ij}||^2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}} = \sqrt{tr{((A-B)^T(A-B))}}.
\end{align}
%
The mean
%
\begin{align}
  F = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i}^{k}(||\Sigma^{\mathcal{G}_0}_i-\Sigma^{\mathcal{G}_1}_i||_F)
\end{align}
%
is taken over pairs of covariances $(\Sigma^{\mathcal{G}_0}_i,\Sigma^{\mathcal{G}_1}_i)$ from each mixture pair $(\mathcal{G}_0,\mathcal{G}_1)$, each composed of $k$ components. In \Cref{fig:frobenius_diffs}, we visualize the mean Frobenius difference between mixture models. We find significant differences in the movement and calibration tasks over subjects, as well as between the calibration task and the trial groups. The divergence between the differences of subsequent trial groups is insignificant, implying that the trial data is itself more self-similar that what we find in the movement and calibration tasks.

We then visualize, in \Cref{fig:frobenius_vs_reward}, the mean over the differences in trial groups for each subject against reward, to understand how the magnitude of the inter-group distance predicts performance. We find that lower performing subjects have exponentially higher mean inter-group distances than higher performing subjects, supporting the idea that higher performing subjects show more self-similarity in their activations than lower performings subjects. We speculate that lower performing subjects tend to maintain variability in an effort to balance exploration and exploitation in the task while higher performing subjects tend to minimize exploration as they attempt to minimize the variability of their solutions. This is in accordance with what we see in the \Cref{fig:gmm_entropy_diffs}, where the mixture models of lower performing subjects tend to maintain their entropy while higher performers tend to decrease entropy across learning.

Our Frobenius method of inter-mixture distance is somewhat unprincipled, in the sense that minimizing the Frobenius distance does not have a unique solution, there are many matrix norms we could have chosen in place of the 2-norm, and the mean of these distances may ``wash out'' effects of the particularities of each mixture model.

For a more well-founded measurement of differences between mixture distributions, we look to the theory of optimal transport. Optimal transport formalizes the problem of finding an transformation between two probability distributions which minimizes a chosen cost function. Given the form of the two probability distributions and the cost, the theory generates an optimal plan in the form of a higher-dimensional probability distribution (the dimensionality determined by the product of the dimensionalities of the input distributions) which captures the magnitude and direction of probability mass to move in order to transform one distribution into another. The optimal transport plan is a geodesic path in the space of distributions along which the cost is minimized, the distance being the total cost incurred for the transport. For Gaussian mixture distributions in particular, a variant of the Wasserstein distance, $MW_2$, was derived to simplify the determination of an optimal transport plan between two mixtures based on the Wasserstein distance $W_2$ between two multivariate Gaussians $\mathcal{N}_0(\mu_0,\Sigma_0)$ and $\mathcal{N}_1(\mu_1,\Sigma_1)$\cite{delonWassersteintypeDistanceSpace2020}. $W_2$ is defined
%
\begin{align}
  W_2^2(\mathcal{N}_0,\mathcal{N}_1) = ||\mu_0-\mu_1||^2 + tr{(\Sigma_0 + \Sigma_1 - 2( {\Sigma_0}^{-1} \Sigma_1 {\Sigma_0}^{-1} )^{-1})}.
\end{align}
%
% https://alexhwilliams.info/itsneuronalblog/2020/10/09/optimal-transport/
$MW_2$ between for two Gaussian mixtures $\mathcal{G}_0$ and $\mathcal{G}_1$ with $k$ and $l$ mixture components ($\mathcal{N}_k$ and $\mathcal{N}_l$), respectively, is
%
\begin{align}
  MW_2^2(\mathcal{G}_0,\mathcal{G}_1) = \min_{w}\sum_{kl}{w_{kl}W_2^2(\mathcal{N}_k,\mathcal{N}_l)}.
\end{align}
%
The distance represents the cost incurred by transporting the mixture model mass along the geodesic defined by the optimal transport plan. 

We use this distance as a way to track progression from early stages to late stages of learning by computing the distance between pairwise mixture models fit to data, as in the Frobenius case. This distance gives us a more intuitive depiction of learning, where subjects incur some effort to shift their policies over time, driven by reward. In \Cref{fig:wasserstein_diffs}, we visualize the Wasserstein differences between pairs of mixture models for each subject. We see a similar pattern as in the Frobenius case, except significant differences between early and late trials when comparing trial group differences. This provides support for the idea that trial groups become more self-similar at the level of individual activity modes. 

We then look to predictions of reward based on inter-mixture differences. In \Cref{fig:wasserstein_vs_reward} we plot the logarithm of the mean inter-mixture Wasserstein distances over the trial group pairs against mean reward for all subjects. We find the a similar result as before, where lower performing subjects tend to display exponentially larger differences on average over learning between their activation distributions as opposed to higher performers. 

One explanation for this trend is that higher performers have lower overall variability in their EMG distributions, and thus have inherently lower magnitude differences in their models over time compared to their lower performing counterparts. We tested this explanation by normalizing mixture differences based on total variance, and found no change in the result. This is supported by \Cref{fig:gmm_entropy_vs_reward} where entropy is linear in reward over subjects while the trend here is exponential. We suggest that there is a process of exploration within the activation space which yields higher differences for lower performing subjects than higher performing subjects. Higher performers appear to discover target solutions and maintain their activation distribution over learning while lower performers appear to change their activation distribution over time in an effort, we speculate, to discover more rewarding task solutions.

\section{Summary}

In this chapter, we use mixture models to peel back the layers of EMG variability across subjects. We discover that subjects tend to decrease variability across their modes of EMG activity. This decrease occurs across the spatial dimensions of EMG to generate solutions with greater self-similarity. Higher-performing subjects show lower entropy and variance in their later target task trials compared to lower-performing subjects. This aligns with the idea that higher performers were able to identify and reliably reproduce rewarding muscle activation patterns, while lower performers continued their exploration of the activation space. The subspace confinement, or effective rank, of subjects' mixture components decreases on average over learning, indicating that the spatial variability of muscle activations becomes more evenly distributed across spatial dimensions rather than more concentrated along a few components. Higher reward is associated with this degree of subspace confinement in the target task trials: more performant subjects are less spatially constrained in their activity. Taken together, these findings paint a picture of subjects engaging in a reward-driven reinforcement learning process, where successful performers are able to balance exploration (generating variability across multiple dimensions of the EMG space) with exploitation (converging on reliable, rewarding muscle activation patterns). Higher-performing individuals are able to more effectively navigate this exploration-exploitation trade-off, ultimately achieving greater self-similarity in their activation distributions and less subspace-confinement. We will explore the relationship between task-relevant and task-irrelevant subspaces of variability to test this hypothesis in the next chapter.
